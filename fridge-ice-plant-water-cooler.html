Applications — fridge, ice plant, water cooler

Thermal Engineering-II · DI05019021 · Domestic refrigerator · Ice cans · Water cooler

What you should be able to do

  • Trace a domestic refrigerator: hermetic compressor, condenser, capillary, evaporator.
  • Explain a can ice plant: primary refrigerant, brine, ice cans, and how the block is released.
  • Distinguish a storage water cooler from an instantaneous one.
  • Say why the condenser is outside the cold space, and why brine must be below 0°C to make ice.

2-minute idea

All three machines are vapour-compression plants from lesson 3.2. They differ in what is being cooled and how the cold reaches it. The condenser is always on the warm side: it rejects the heat taken from the product plus the compressor work, so it must not sit inside the cold space.

A domestic refrigerator is a small sealed system. A hermetic compressor (motor and compressor in one welded shell) pushes vapour into an air-cooled condenser on the back. Liquid passes a filter-drier and a capillary tube, which is the expansion device: a long thin tube with no moving parts. The refrigerant boils in a plate evaporator in the freezer. The freezer air is kept well below 0°C, commonly about −18°C for frozen food. The fresh-food space stays above freezing, commonly below about 5°C. The evaporator surface is colder than that air, or heat would not flow into the coil. A thermostat stops the compressor when the cabinet is cold enough. The sealed charge is a refrigerant such as R-134a or, on many newer fridges, R-600a (isobutane). The older CFC R-12 is not used in new machines.

A can ice plant freezes water in metal cans hung in a tank of cold brine (a solution of common salt or calcium chloride). Brine is a secondary coolant. It stays liquid below 0°C and carries cold from the evaporator coils to the cans. The primary refrigerant, often ammonia (R-717) on a large plant, boils in those coils. It does not flow through the cans. Ice grows from the can wall inward. Cans are then dipped briefly in warm water so the block releases. For clear ice, air is bubbled through the water while it freezes, and the unfrozen core, which holds the impurities, is sucked out. The plant uses an expansion valve, not a domestic capillary. The compressor is a large one, often open type.

A water cooler cools drinking water but must not freeze it. A storage cooler keeps a tank cold and you draw from that store. An instantaneous cooler cools the water as it flows through a coil or a heat exchanger when the tap is opened, with little water stored. Both are usually small air-cooled vapour-compression units.

Common mistakes

  • Mistake: “Brine is the refrigerant in an ice plant.” — Brine is only the secondary coolant. The primary refrigerant, often ammonia, boils in the coils. The cans never carry that refrigerant.
  • Mistake: “A domestic fridge uses the same expansion valve as an ice plant.” — A simple domestic fridge uses a capillary tube. A large ice plant uses an expansion valve.
  • Mistake: “The condenser belongs inside the freezer.” — The condenser must reject heat to the room. It is on the outside. If it were inside, it would heat the food.
  • Mistake: “The air in the freezer is at the refrigerant’s boiling temperature.” — Heat flows only from warmer air to a colder coil. The evaporator surface is colder than the air set-point. Frozen-food air is commonly about −18°C; the coil is colder than that.
