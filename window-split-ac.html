Window and split AC

Thermal Engineering-II · DI05019021 · Window unit · Split unit · TR · EER

What you should be able to do

  • Place the compressor, condenser, expansion device and evaporator in a window unit and in a split unit.
  • Say what happens to room air at the indoor coil, and where the heat is rejected.
  • Convert a tonnage into kJ/min, kW and Btu/h with the course values of 1 TR.
  • Write cooling EER as output kW divided by electrical input kW, and compare it with a Carnot ceiling.

2-minute idea

A room air conditioner is the vapour-compression cycle of lesson 3.2, applied to air. Room air passes over the evaporator. The coil is colder than the dew point, so the process is cooling and dehumidification from lesson 4.2: dry-bulb temperature falls and water condenses. The condenser sits outdoors and rejects that heat plus the compressor work. The condenser must not face the room.

A window AC puts every part in one box fitted in a window or a wall opening. The evaporator and its fan face the room. The condenser, condenser fan and hermetic compressor face outside. A capillary tube is the usual expansion device. Water that drips off the evaporator is often thrown onto the condenser by a slinger on the outdoor fan, which helps the condenser. The compressor is in the same box as the room, so the unit is noisier indoors. One motor often turns both fans.

A split AC separates the box. The indoor unit has the evaporator, air filter and blower. The outdoor unit has the compressor, air-cooled condenser and its fan. Two refrigerant pipes join them through a small hole: a smaller liquid line and a larger suction line. The suction line is cold, so it is insulated. A drain pipe takes condensate away. The compressor is outside, so the room is quieter. The window can stay closed. Many new units use R-32; older ones often used R-22.

Capacity is still quoted in ton of refrigeration. From lesson 3.1, 1 TR = 211 kJ/min = 12000 Btu/h. In kilowatts, divide kJ/min by 60, so 1 TR is 211/60 = 3.517 kW, written 3.52 kW when rounded to two decimals. EER here means cooling output divided by electrical input, both in kW. The same ratio in Btu/h per watt is about 3.412 times larger, because 1 kW is about 3412 Btu/h. A real EER must stay below the Carnot COP between the cold evaporator and the hot condenser. Those coil temperatures are lower and higher than the room air and the outdoor air.

Common mistakes

  • Mistake: “The indoor unit of a split AC contains the compressor.” — The indoor unit is the evaporator and blower. The compressor is in the outdoor unit, with the condenser.
  • Mistake: “1 TR = 211 kW, or 3.52 kJ/min.” — 1 TR = 211 kJ/min = 12000 Btu/h. Divide by 60 to get 3.517 kW, which rounds to 3.52 kW.
  • Mistake: “An EER of 3.5 kW/kW is the same figure as 3.5 Btu/h per watt.” — Btu/h per watt is about 3.412 times the kW/kW ratio, because 1 kW is about 3412 Btu/h. An EER of 3.52 kW/kW is about 12 Btu/h per watt.
  • Mistake: “The condenser cools the room.” — The evaporator cools and dehumidifies room air. The condenser dumps heat outside. That heat is the room load plus the compressor work.
